Use double spaces after sentences.

Use commas like in normal English.

Prefer "Let" or "Write" over "Put".

Use "Let ... be" to introduce a universal quantifier.  Use "Let ... denote" or "Denote by ...", etc., to introduce notation.

Put spaces and braces between TeX symbols the same way that I do when I write papers; see ~/doit/ for plenty of examples.  For example, use "\mathcal{A}" over "\mathcal A", "\hat{\varphi}" over "\hat\varphi"; for measures, put spaces after the "d", e.g., "\int f(x) \, d x".

Try to keep indentation like what AUCTeX does.

In introductions and strategy summaries, prioritize the conceptual argument over proof-level precision.  Do not add technical qualifications about normalization, auxiliary parameters, or exceptional cases unless they are needed to understand the idea.  For example, saying that amplification gives a "saving" need not specify "normalized by the target amplifier eigenvalue": the intended normalization can remain implicit at this level.  Keep such details in the formal statements and proofs.  When reviewing introductory prose, distinguish a materially misleading claim from harmless shorthand, and preserve the latter.
